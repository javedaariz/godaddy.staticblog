## The new rates

The Budget on 28 October 2026 added a further two percentage points to the rises announced a year earlier. The result for 2027-28:

| Taxpayer | Savings rate now | From 6 April 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic rate | 20% | 24% |
| Higher rate | 40% | 44% |
| Additional rate | 45% | 49% |

The rates apply across the whole UK. The changes are to be legislated in Finance Bill 2026/27, so check GOV.UK for the final text.

## What stays the same

- The **Personal Savings Allowance** is unchanged: normally £1,000 of interest tax-free for basic-rate taxpayers, £500 for higher-rate taxpayers and nothing for additional-rate taxpayers.
- The **0% starting rate for savings** is unchanged.
- Interest inside an **ISA** is still tax-free.

## Worked examples

These use round numbers for illustration, assuming interest outside an ISA and no other savings income.

**Basic-rate taxpayer with £1,500 of interest.** The first £1,000 is covered by the allowance. The remaining £500 is taxed at 24%, which is £120. At today's 20% it would be £100, so £20 more.

**Higher-rate taxpayer with £1,500 of interest.** The first £500 is covered. The remaining £1,000 is taxed at 44%, which is £440. At today's 40% it would be £400, so £40 more.

**Additional-rate taxpayer with £1,500 of interest.** There is no allowance, so all £1,500 is taxed at 49%, which is £735. At today's 45% it would be £675, so £60 more.

For scale, £10,000 earning 4% interest is £400 a year, which is within the allowance for most basic and higher-rate taxpayers.

## What to consider

- If your interest is close to your allowance, the higher rate makes using an ISA more worthwhile. The [cash ISA limit has stayed at £20,000](/money/cash-isa-limit-stays-20000/).
- Interest is taxed in the year you earn it, not when you withdraw it.
- Check whether your bank pays interest gross, and whether you need to report it on a Self Assessment return.

This is general information. For decisions about your own savings, speak to a regulated adviser or accountant.
